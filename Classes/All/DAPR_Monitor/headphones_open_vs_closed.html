Headphones: Open-Back vs Closed-Back

Your headphones are one of the biggest variables in how accurately you hear your own mix. This page covers what to buy, what to avoid, and what small accessory you will need on day one so your headphones actually plug into the interfaces in the lab and at home.

Open-Back vs Closed-Back: The Short Version

  • Open-back earcups let air pass through the back of the driver. This produces a wider, more natural soundstage that is closer to listening on studio monitors in a treated room. Sound leaks out and outside sound leaks in, so these are for mixing and critical listening in a quiet room, never for tracking.
  • Closed-back earcups are sealed. Nothing leaks out, so vocals and instruments being recorded do not bleed into the microphone. The tradeoff is a narrower, more boxed-in soundstage after long sessions. Use closed-back for tracking, recording, and any environment with background noise.
Wireless and Bluetooth headphones: do not mix on these

Bluetooth headphones compress audio through a codec (SBC, AAC, or aptX) and introduce real, measurable latency, often 100 to 300 milliseconds between the signal leaving your interface and the sound reaching your ears. That delay alone makes them unusable for tracking with monitoring, and lossy compression reshapes the frequency response you are trying to judge. Always work wired, straight into your interface, for every mixing and tracking assignment in this course.

Noise-cancelling headphones and earbuds: not for mixing

Active noise cancellation works by generating an inverted signal and blending it back into what you hear. That process actively reshapes the frequency response and phase, especially in the low end, which is exactly the range you are trying to judge honestly. Earbuds add a second problem: their bass response changes drastically depending on how deeply and consistently they seal in your ear canal, so two people wearing the same earbuds can hear two different mixes. Neither product type belongs in a mixing workflow. Use full-size, wired, non-ANC headphones for every assignment.

You need a 1/4 inch adapter

Studio interfaces use a 1/4 inch (6.35mm) TRS headphone jack. There is no 1/8 inch (3.5mm) headphone output on the interfaces in the lab. Every headphone listed on this page ships with a 1/8 inch plug, so you need a 1/4 inch adapter unless the specific pair you buy already includes one. These adapters cost about five dollars. Buy one and keep it in your gig bag.

Open-Back and Semi-Open Recommendations (Mixing and Mastering)

Use open-back headphones for mixing, mastering, and any critical listening pass, always in a quiet room. Do not use these for tracking; sound will leak into any nearby microphone.

A quick word on impedance

Impedance is measured in ohms and tells you how much electrical resistance a headphone puts up against the signal coming from your amp or interface. It is a major reason the same headphone model can behave differently depending on what you plug it into, and it is why the AKG K240 line changed dramatically over the decades. See the K240 entry below for that story.

Low impedance (roughly 16 to 80 ohms): needs less voltage to reach full volume, so a phone, laptop headphone jack, or a basic interface headphone output can drive it loud enough with no extra gear. Most headphones designed for everyday studio and home use sit in this range on purpose, including nearly every model recommended on this page.

High impedance (150 to 600 ohms): needs significantly more voltage to reach the same volume. These headphones historically paired with the high-voltage, low-current outputs used in broadcast and professional gear, and they tend to reject electrical noise and interference better once properly driven. The tradeoff is that a phone or a weak headphone jack simply cannot push enough voltage to make them loud, which is why high-impedance headphones are usually paired with a dedicated headphone amplifier.

For every assignment in this course, stick with the low-impedance range unless you already own a headphone amp. Every model in the table below, including the current AKG K240 Studio, can be plugged straight into the lab interfaces and driven to a comfortable working volume with no amp required. That was not always true of the K240 name, which is exactly why the impedance rating matters when you shop for a used or vintage pair.

Philips SHP9500
Budget entry point. Comfortable for long sessions and surprisingly open sounding for the price. A good first pair if you are not ready to commit to a pricier reference headphone.
Audio-Technica ATH-R30x
A purpose-built open-back reference headphone at a student-friendly price. Lightweight, replaceable velour pads, and a wide, detailed soundstage.
Sennheiser HD 560S
Neutral, well-regarded reference tuning. Low enough impedance to sound good straight out of an interface without a separate headphone amp.
Beyerdynamic DT 880 Pro (250-ohm)
A long-standing studio staple. Detailed and revealing enough for serious mixing work. The 250-ohm version benefits from a dedicated headphone amp if your interface has a weak headphone output.
Sennheiser HD 490 Pro
The top pick in this lineup and near the top of the budget range below. Ships with two swappable earpad sets tuned for mixing versus general production, plus a bundled room-simulation plugin.
AKG K240 Studio

A studio classic that has been in continuous production in one form or another since the 1970s. The original AKG K240 and the later K240 Monitor were both 600-ohm headphones, built for the high-voltage, low-current headphone outputs found on broadcast consoles and professional studio gear of that era. Reaching a comfortable volume on those older models requires real amplifier power, which is exactly why vintage 600-ohm K240 units sound quiet and underwhelming when plugged into a phone or laptop.

The K240 Studio sold today is a different animal electrically. AKG re-engineered it down to 55 ohms specifically so it could be driven directly from mixers, interfaces, and portable gear without a separate headphone amp, while keeping the same semi-open, hammock-suspension headband design the model is known for. It is one of the least expensive semi-open headphones you can buy that still has a real studio pedigree, which makes it a reasonable first pair or a reliable backup.

Closed-Back Recommendations (Tracking and Recording)

Use closed-back headphones any time a microphone is live in the room, or any time you need isolation from outside noise. These seal against your ear, so nothing leaks out into the mic and nothing leaks in from the room.

Sony MDR-7506
The industry-standard tracking headphone. Folding design, coiled cable, and strong isolation for the price. You will see this exact pair in nearly every professional studio.
Sennheiser HD 280 Pro
Excellent passive isolation, a rotating earcup for one-ear monitoring, and a durable build meant for daily studio use.
Audio-Technica ATH-M50x
The most widely used closed-back studio headphone in the world. Detachable cables, comfortable clamping force, and a slightly boosted bass and treble that make it easy to listen to for hours.
Beyerdynamic DT 770 Pro (80-ohm)
Plush velour pads and a warmer low end make this a favorite for long tracking sessions. The 80-ohm version drives easily from a standard interface headphone output.
Shure SRH840A
A step up from the M50x with a flatter, more reference-leaning tuning. Detachable cable and replaceable pads for long-term durability.

Headphones vs. Monitors: Strengths and Tradeoffs

Headphones and studio monitors are not interchangeable tools. Each one tells you something true about your mix and hides something else. Understand what each one is good at, and use both.

Headphones: strengths
  • Work anywhere. A dorm room, a shared apartment, or a late-night session with people asleep down the hall are all fine, since nothing leaks into the room.
  • No room to fight. An untreated bedroom cannot lie to you through headphones the way it can through speakers, since there are no room reflections involved.
  • Excellent for detail work. Catching clicks, pops, breath sounds, low-level noise, and sibilance is often easier with the isolation and close-up detail headphones provide.
  • Lower cost of entry. A single pair of headphones gets you working; a monitor setup needs stands, cables, and ideally some acoustic treatment to perform as intended.
Headphones: tradeoffs
  • No crossfeed. In a room, both ears hear both speakers, which is how the brain naturally builds a stereo image. In headphones, the left ear only ever hears the left channel, which exaggerates stereo width and can make panning and width decisions misleading.
  • Unreliable bass judgment. Because the driver sits directly against or inside the ear, bass response is amplified in a way that will not match what a listener hears from any speaker, phone, or car system.
  • Faster listening fatigue. Long sessions in headphones tire the ears sooner than the same session on speakers in open air.
  • No feedback on translation. Headphones cannot tell you how a mix will hold up on the wildly different playback systems most listeners will actually use.
Monitors: strengths
  • Natural crossfeed. Both ears hear both speakers, just like every real-world listening situation, which makes stereo width and panning decisions trustworthy.
  • Better translation. A mix that sounds right on monitors is far more likely to sound right in a car, on a phone speaker, or through a club system, since monitors reproduce sound the way listeners actually experience it.
  • Lower fatigue over long sessions. Sound arriving through open air is easier on the ears than sound clamped directly against them for hours.
  • Reveals true low-end behavior. Bass response through monitors, in a reasonably treated room, is a far more honest picture than headphone bass.
Monitors: tradeoffs
  • Completely room-dependent. An untreated room adds standing waves, comb filtering, and bass buildup in corners that can make a monitor's own accuracy irrelevant. A great pair of monitors in a bad room can mislead you worse than mediocre headphones.
  • Require a quiet, controlled space. Shared housing, thin walls, and late-night schedules make monitor-based mixing impractical for many students.
  • Higher total cost. Stands, cabling, and any acoustic treatment add up fast on top of the monitors themselves.
  • Not usable while tracking. Speaker output bleeds into any live microphone in the room, so monitors are a mixing and mastering tool, not a tracking tool.
The practical takeaway
Use headphones to catch fine detail and to work when a quiet, treated room is not available. Make final decisions on stereo width, panning, and low end on monitors, in as controlled a room as you can manage. Before calling a mix finished, check it on a third, deliberately mediocre reference such as a phone speaker or the Avantone MixCube described below, since that is closer to how most listeners will actually hear it.

10 Studio Monitors: Budget to Mastering-Grade

Monitors are priced and sold as a pair unless noted. This list spans a full range, from a first desktop pair to mastering-grade reference monitors, so you can see what the jump in price actually buys.

PreSonus Eris E3.5
The cheapest real entry point into monitoring. Small 3.5-inch drivers mean thin bass, but the stereo imaging and midrange accuracy are honest for the price. A reasonable first pair for a small desk.
JBL 305P MkII
The most widely owned monitor in this list and a genuine step up from desktop-tier speakers. JBL's waveguide gives an unusually wide, even sweet spot, so the mix sounds consistent even if you are not sitting dead center.
Yamaha HS5
The direct descendant of the legendary NS-10, still the reference point most engineers compare other monitors against. The sound is deliberately unflattering: if a mix sounds good on the HS5, it tends to sound good everywhere. Choose this over the KRK Rokit 5 G4 below if you want a diagnostic tool rather than a fun-to-listen-to speaker.
KRK Rokit 5 G4
A bass-forward alternative to the HS5, popular with hip-hop and electronic producers who want to hear low end that is closer to how it will sound on club and car systems. The tradeoff for that extra bass is a less neutral picture of the overall mix. Choose this over the HS5 if your genre lives in the low end and you already know how to compensate for a hyped bottom.
Adam Audio T5V
Adam's ribbon-style tweeter gives noticeably clearer, more detailed highs than either the HS5 or the Rokit 5 G4, which makes decisions like de-essing and cymbal balance faster and more confident. The tradeoff is a slightly more sculpted low end than the HS5, which can mask a thin kick or a muddy bass part. Choose this over the HS5 if you spend long hours mixing vocals and want less ear fatigue at the top end.
Yamaha HS8
The same HS-series tuning as the HS5, in a cabinet with an 8-inch woofer that reaches real low end without a separate subwoofer. Choose this over the HS5 once your room is large enough to support it and you are working with bass-heavy material regularly, since a bigger woofer in a small untreated room can cause more bass buildup than it solves.
Focal Alpha 65 Evo
French-made monitors with a distinctive, exceptionally clear high end and a wide sweet spot that stays consistent even off-axis. Many engineers describe them as more engaging to listen to than the Yamaha HS series while remaining accurate enough to mix on. A strong choice once your budget clears the sub-$500 tier and you want a monitor that also doubles as an enjoyable everyday listening speaker.
Genelec 8010A
A tiny 3-inch driver in a cast-aluminum enclosure that still delivers genuine professional-grade accuracy, widely used by film and broadcast engineers who need a compact, grab-and-go monitor. Choose this over the larger monitors on this list if desk space is the limiting factor rather than budget, since Genelec charges a premium for that small footprint.
Neumann KH 120 II
The ceiling of this list. Onboard DSP allows the monitor to correct its own response for room placement, and the build quality and resolution are what most professional mastering rooms are built around. Choose this only once every other link in the chain, room treatment included, is good enough to actually reveal what this monitor can do.
Avantone Active MixCube
Not a monitor you mix on. A single full-range driver with no crossover deliberately recreates the sound of a cheap TV, clock radio, or laptop speaker, so you can check whether a mix still reads clearly once the highs and lows are gone. If a vocal or kick drum gets lost on the MixCube, it will get lost on a phone speaker too. Use this alongside a real pair of monitors, never instead of one.
