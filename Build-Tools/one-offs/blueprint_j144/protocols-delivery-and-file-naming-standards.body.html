Delivery and File Naming Standards

When a session leaves your hands, someone else has to open it, find the right mix and know exactly what each file is, years from now. The Recording Academy Producers & Engineers Wing publishes free recommendations for exactly that: how to name mixes and stems, how to organize a project folder, and what a finished project should hand over. Studios, labels and mastering engineers use them, so learning them now means your files already look professional.

Naming a mix or stem file

A delivery file name carries everything about the file, in a fixed order, separated by underscores:

Parts of a delivery file name
PartWhat goes thereExample
Artist initialsUsually two letters, from the artist's first and last nameJS
Song titleEach word capitalized, no spaces, punctuation or accents; under 15 characters if you canHorseNotHome
Mixer initials and mix numberWho mixed it and which revision, two digitsAW03
Version or stemWhat the file is: Master, VocalUp, NoLeadVocal, Instrumental, or a stem name such as DrumsVocalUp
Sample rate and bit depthThe letter k for kilohertz, then the bits44k16 or 96k24
ExtensionAlways shown; the only period in the whole name.wav

Put together: JS_HorseNotHome_AW03_VocalUp_44k16.wav. Anyone can read it at a glance, it sorts correctly, and it opens on every operating system.

Dates in names
When a file or folder name needs a date, use the ISO order, year then month then day: 2026-10-07 or 20261007. It sorts in time order everywhere.

The standard mix delivery

A finished song is more than one mix. The recommended delivery is five versions of the same approved mix:

Minimal mix delivery
VersionWhat it is
MasterThe approved mix, everything in
Vocal UpThe same mix with the lead vocal raised a little
No Lead VocalEverything except the lead vocal; backing vocals stay (once called the TV track)
InstrumentalNo vocals at all
Lead Vocal Only (wet)The lead vocal alone, with its effects

Stems are mixes of one group from the master mix, with all of its processing, panning and effects: drums, bass, guitars, keys, vocals and so on. They get reused for live playback, remixes, games and film. Two things to remember: added together, stems are not a replacement for the master mix, and making them is extra work that should be agreed and paid for before mixing starts.

Organizing a project folder

The recommended hierarchy keeps recording, mixing and mastering separate, so nothing gets lost or overwritten:

Project folder hierarchy
FolderWhat goes in it
Artist, then Project_ProducerOne folder per artist, one per project inside it
Multitracks, then ArtistInitials_SongTitleThe DAW session and its audio for each song, plus Source Files and Exported Files folders
MixesReference Mixes (everything you sent out), Master Mixes (every version and recall), Approved Mixes (only what the client approved), Stems
Mastered MixesOne folder per format: Hi Res, CD, Vinyl, Video
No aliases or shortcuts
Never put aliases, shortcuts or links in a delivery folder. Copy the real files in, consolidate the audio, and check the session opens on another computer before you hand it over.

Formats that last

  • Broadcast WAV (.wav) for every multitrack and stereo file, consolidated from the start of the song so every file lines up.
  • Interleaved stereo files for stereo mixes.
  • Keep the session sample rate and bit depth; a backup is never at a lower resolution than the original.
  • Keep two backups on two different kinds of storage, plus all the paperwork: credits, lyrics, charts and mix notes.

In our classes

Class hand-ins keep the course pattern, Lastname, Firstname - Assignment Name, so Canvas and your instructor can sort them by student. Use the professional naming above for everything inside your projects and sessions, and for any work you deliver to a client, an artist or a studio.
