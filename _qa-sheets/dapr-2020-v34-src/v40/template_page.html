Distortion: What Is Actually Happening

Distortion is a change in the shape of the waveform. Every distortion box, plugin and pedal does the same basic job: it bends the wave. Once you understand what that bending adds, you can predict what any distortion will do before you insert it.

Clipping and waveshaping

Every amplifier, converter and plugin can only swing so far. When the input asks for more than the circuit can deliver, the tops of the wave get squashed or cut off. That is clipping. Waveshaping is the general name for any process that maps each input value to a new output value through a curve, called a transfer curve. A clipper is simply a waveshaper whose curve bends over at the top and the bottom.

Here is the key fact. A pure sine wave contains one frequency. Change its shape at all and you create new frequencies. When the same change happens on every cycle, the new frequencies land at whole number multiples of the original. Those are harmonics. A 100 Hz sine that gets clipped comes out with new energy at 200 Hz, 300 Hz, 400 Hz and up. On a single note, distortion adds harmonics above the fundamental, never below it.

Soft clipping versus hard clipping

Soft clipping bends the peaks gradually. Small signals pass almost untouched, and the curve flattens more as the level climbs. Hard clipping is a straight line right up to a ceiling and then a flat wall. The difference you hear is how far up the spectrum the new harmonics reach.

Soft clipping adds harmonics that fall away quickly, so most of the new energy sits in the low order harmonics close to the fundamental. It sounds thicker and warmer rather than obviously distorted. Hard clipping cuts sharp corners into the wave, and a sharp corner can only be drawn with strong high harmonics. The added energy reaches far up the spectrum, so hard clipping sounds brighter, buzzier and harsher.

Even and odd harmonics

Which harmonics appear depends on symmetry. If a circuit clips the top and the bottom of the wave the same way, it produces only odd harmonics: the 3rd, 5th, 7th and so on. If it treats the top and bottom differently, for example a single tube stage that runs out of room sooner on one side, it adds even harmonics as well: the 2nd, 4th, 6th and so on.

The 2nd harmonic is one octave above the fundamental and the 4th is two octaves above, so even harmonics blend into the note and tend to sound warm, full and musical. The 3rd harmonic is an octave and a fifth up, still consonant, but the 5th, 7th and higher odd harmonics sit further from the notes of the scale, so strong odd order content tends to sound edgy, aggressive or harsh. Treat this as a tendency, not a law. A little 3rd harmonic from tape or a transformer sounds great, and heavy even order distortion can still sound nasty. How far up the harmonics reach matters as much as whether they are even or odd.

Input level and gain staging drive it

A distortion stage does not care what its knob is called. What matters is how hard the signal hits the curve. Turn up the input, or feed it a hotter track, and more of each cycle lands on the bent part of the curve, so more harmonics come out. Turn the input down and the same plugin gets cleaner. That means every level change upstream changes the character of every nonlinear stage downstream. If you raise a vocal 6 dB with clip gain before a saturation plugin, you did not just make it louder, you made it more distorted.

This is why distortion plugins have separate drive and output controls. Drive sets how much the signal distorts. Output only sets how loud the result is. After you set the drive, use the output control to bring the level back to where it was, so you judge the tone and not the volume.

Louder fools you. Added harmonics increase perceived loudness without raising peak level. Clipping also flattens the peaks, so a distorted signal can read the same or lower on a peak meter and still sound louder. Match loudness before you decide the distorted version is better.

Intermodulation distortion: chords versus single notes

Feed a single note into a distortion and every new frequency is a harmonic of that note, so it still sounds like one note, only thicker. Feed it two or more notes at once and the circuit also creates sum and difference frequencies between them. This is intermodulation distortion. Play 200 Hz and 300 Hz together through a clipper and you also get 100 Hz, 500 Hz and a web of other products.

With simple intervals those products line up with the notes. A root and a fifth have a 3:2 ratio, so their difference tone lands an octave below the root, which is why power chords survive heavy gain and even sound bigger. A full major or minor chord contains thirds that do not line up so neatly, especially in equal temperament, so the intermodulation products land between the notes and the chord turns into mud and beating. When a heavily distorted chord sounds like mush, the fix is fewer notes or less gain, not more EQ.

Test with a single note, then a chord. If a single note sounds great and a full chord sounds muddy, you are hearing intermodulation. Back off the drive or simplify the voicing before you reach for an EQ.
