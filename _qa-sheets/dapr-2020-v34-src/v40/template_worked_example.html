Comping and Arrangement: Worked Example, Comping a Lead Vocal from Five Takes

This page walks through one complete comp from start to finish. The session is a verse and chorus of lead vocal, six lines long, recorded as five full takes on alternate playlists of the same track. The goal is one performance on the main playlist that sounds like the singer on their best day, with no join the listener can find.

Start with an empty comp playlist. Choose New from the track Playlist selector and name the new playlist Lead Comp. It becomes the main playlist, and the five takes stay underneath as alternate playlists.

Step 1: Open Playlists view

Choose Playlists from the Track View selector on the vocal track. The main playlist sits at the top, and every alternate playlist appears as a lane below it. Each lane has a Solo Lane button and a Copy Selection to Main Playlist button. Rename lanes such as Lead Vox.03 to Take 1 through Take 5 so your notes match the screen.

Step 2: Make two listening passes

Pass one is for the whole performance. Play each take top to bottom without looking at the screen and write one sentence about it, such as best energy, tired, or pitchy chorus. Do not rate anything yet.

Pass two is phrase by phrase. Select line 1, turn on Loop Playback from the Options menu, and use the Solo Lane buttons to hear each take of that line in turn while the loop runs. The band keeps playing underneath, because a line that sounds fine alone can drag or fight the guitar in context. Move to line 2 only when line 1 has a rating on every take.

Rating the takes

Rate every phrase of every take from 1 to 5. If you separate each take into phrase clips first with Edit > Separate Clip > At Selection, you can rate each clip with Clip > Rating and use the Filter Lanes menu to hide lanes that fall below a rating. A grid on paper works just as well. Either way, it is the record of your decisions.

What each rating means in this example
RatingMeaning
5Keeper. Pitch, timing and feeling are all right.
4Strong. Wins whenever no 5 exists for that line.
3Usable with work. One small flaw, such as a flat long vowel.
2Backup only. A clear pitch or timing problem.
1Unusable. Wrong lyric, a cracked note, or noise.

Read the grid by column. Take 4 owns line 1, take 3 owns lines 2 and 5, take 5 owns lines 3 and 6, and take 2 owns line 4. Line 5 has no 5 anywhere, so the best available is the 4 from take 3. When a column tops out at 3 or lower, that line needs another recorded pass, not more editing.

Step 3: Copy each winning phrase to the main playlist

With the Selector, drag across line 1 on the Take 4 lane, starting in the silence before the first word and ending in the silence after the last. Click Copy Selection to Main Playlist on that lane, or press Shift+Option+Up Arrow on a Mac (Shift+Alt+Up Arrow on Windows). The phrase lands on the main playlist at exactly the same timeline position, so its timing against the band does not change. Repeat for every column of the grid.

Step 4: Choose the cut points

Where a selection starts and ends decides whether anyone hears the join. Two places hide edits well.

Before the breath. When a line starts with an audible breath, cut before the breath, not between the breath and the first word. The breath carries the tone, distance and energy of the take it came from, so it belongs with the line that follows it. A breath from take 1 followed by words from take 3 sounds like the singer stepped closer to the microphone halfway through one inhale.

Just before a hard consonant. Inside a line, the safest cut is in the short silence right before a consonant such as p, t, k or b. The sharp attack of the consonant masks the change of take. Avoid cutting inside a held vowel when a consonant is nearby, because the vowel exposes every difference between takes.

Step 5: Crossfade every join with the Smart Tool

Turn on the Smart Tool. Hold the pointer near the bottom of the boundary between two clips until the crossfade cursor appears, then drag across the boundary to draw the crossfade. Keep it short at consonants, around 5 to 10 milliseconds, and longer in sustained material. If Pro Tools will not create the fade, one clip has no audio beyond its edge to fade into; move the boundary slightly so both sides have material.

Step 6: Match levels with clip gain

Takes are rarely recorded at the same level, so a phrase from take 2 can arrive noticeably quieter than the take 4 phrase before it. Fix level jumps with clip gain, not the track fader, so every plugin on the track receives an even signal. Choose View > Clip > Clip Gain Info to show the small clip gain fader in the corner of each clip, then click it and drag. Match by ear with the loop running until the change of take disappears.

The order matters. A compressor fed an uneven comp works harder on the loud phrases than on the quiet ones, so the jump survives compression with a different tone on each side of the join.

Step 7: Check the comp in context

When every line is in, turn off lane solo and loop, bring the band up at mix level, and play the section twice. On the first pass, close your eyes and listen for anything that pulls your attention to an edit: a tone shift, a clipped breath, a vowel that changes color. On the second pass, drop a marker on any join you doubted and fix it. A comp that only works soloed is not finished.

Choose, do not nudge. If a line is late, choose a different take for it or note it for a separate timing pass. Moving phrases while you comp mixes two jobs together and hides the choice you made.
